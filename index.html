The 25th Reunion Issue · Class of 2001

Where Are They Now?

Twenty-five years after they last stumbled across the Quiet Green at 3 a.m., the Class of 2001 returns to College Hill. We tracked down three classmates who, in their own spectacularly Brown ways, decided that the road taken should never be the obvious one.

By The Editors · Caricatures by The Studio
I.

The Man Who Knows the Moss

Theodore “Teddy” Whitcomb-Hayes ’01 — Independent Concentration in Computational Bryolichenology — has spent a quarter century proving that the world’s most important organism is one almost no one has heard of.

“People keep asking what moss is for. That’s the wrong question. Moss is for us, the same way the ocean is for fish: it just happens to also be there.”

— Whitcomb-Hayes, in The Atlantic, 2024

You first meet Teddy at the end of a half-mile dirt drive in Glover, Vermont, where a hand-routed sign reads NEKICS — Knock Twice, Then Wait. He has not, in fact, been waiting for you. He has been waiting for a particular bead of dew on a particular shoot of Sphagnum capillifolium, which obliges him at 9:14 a.m. He nods, satisfied, and only then notices the reporter.

As an undergraduate, Whitcomb-Hayes designed his own concentration after a freshman seminar with Professor Susan Alcock convinced him that the future of computation lay not in silicon but in the symbiotic mat. Brown’s Open Curriculum, he says, “was the only place that didn’t ask me to apologize.” A senior thesis on lichen as a model for distributed memory drew polite confusion from his readers and a small grant from a Norwegian foundation no one had heard of. He flew to Svalbard the week after Commencement and stayed for six years.

What followed is the kind of career path that does not exist on LinkedIn: a postdoc in Tromsø; a stint at the Missouri Botanical Garden cataloguing pressings from a 19th-century eccentric; two seasons as a guide on Isle Royale; a brief, well-publicized arrest in Tasmania for “unauthorized cushion-plant photography.” In 2014 he came home to Vermont, bought a sugar-shack and three acres of bog, and started the Northeast Kingdom Institute for Cryptogamic Studies. NEKICS has one employee. He has, to date, named four moss species and one slime mold, the last of which he insisted on calling Physarum brunense.

He will, he confirms, attend Reunion — provided his ride from the Park-and-Ride doesn’t mind a cooler of live specimens in the back seat.

II.

The Senator from Ward 14

Marisol “Mars” Reyes-Delacroix ’01 — Ethnic Studies & Political Science — was last seen by most classmates blocking the entrance to University Hall in November of 2000. She has been blocking something ever since.

“I was radicalized by a Brown housing seminar and the meal-plan negotiations of 1999. I have been radicalized by every meal plan since.”

— Reyes-Delacroix, on the floor of the R.I. Senate, 2025

The night before she filed her bill to cap rents in census tracts where median income has fallen for three consecutive years, Senator Reyes-Delacroix slept four hours on a couch in a Pawtucket triple-decker that, by her own count, was the eighteenth she had stayed in this year. “If I’m going to write the law,” she says, “I’d like to be sitting on the floor when I do it.”

Classmates will remember Mars as the one with the megaphone — outside University Hall during the spring 1999 occupation, on the steps of the Rock during Mumia week, and, most famously, in Seattle in November of her senior fall, where she ducked out of midterms with three other Brown students and a borrowed Subaru to attend the WTO protests. (She returned to campus with a sprained wrist, a press pass from a magazine that did not exist, and an A-minus.)

After graduation she organized janitors in Los Angeles for SEIU, then home healthcare workers in central Massachusetts, then food-service staff across the entire Rhode Island hospital system. She ran for Providence City Council in 2013 on a budget of $4,700, unseated a four-term incumbent, and discovered she enjoyed reading bond documents. A state senate seat followed in 2018. Last year her tenant-protection package passed both chambers; this year the governor signed it under protest, which she counts as a win.

“I’m more boring than I used to be,” she insists. “I read a lot of actuarial tables now.” Then she stops, considers, and laughs. “Okay, I’m exactly as annoying as I used to be. I just have staff.”

III.

The Lighthouse Keeper of the Avant-Garde

Beatrix “Bex” Underhill ’01 — double concentration in Modern Culture & Media and Literary Arts — makes films almost no one sees and writes poems almost everyone, eventually, reads.

“A poem is a film you watch with your tongue. A film is a poem you watch with your shoulders. I don’t see a difference and I’ve stopped pretending I do.”

— Underhill, MacArthur Foundation interview, 2023

It is the third take of a shot in which nothing happens: a fog bank, a beam of light, a gull. Bex Underhill is sitting cross-legged on a wet rock with a notebook, writing what may or may not be a poem and what may or may not become the voice-over. “The water,” she says, not looking up, “rhymes with everything if you give it enough time.”

At Brown she co-edited The Hovel, a one-issue literary magazine printed on the backs of dining-hall placemats; performed in three Production Workshop plays, all of which involved at least one bathtub onstage; and made a thesis film in 16mm about her grandmother’s hands. The film was screened once, in a Carmichael basement, to eleven people and one fire marshal. It is now in the permanent collection of the Whitney.

The decade after Brown was, in her telling, “a string of residencies and coffee jobs in roughly equal measure.” MacDowell, Yaddo, Skowhegan, Civitella Ranieri. A first chapbook in 2007. A short at Sundance in 2011. A debut feature, Bell Buoy, that screened in seventeen cities and earned back its budget by $400. A second collection of poems, The Reef Asks for Nothing, that won the National Book Critics Circle Award. In 2023 the MacArthur Foundation called; she thought it was a scam and let it go to voicemail.

Eleven Lighthouses, her fourth feature, premieres at the New York Film Festival in September. The first lighthouse is, of course, Point Judith. She has promised the editors a Reunion-week screening at the Avon, “weather and ferry schedule permitting.”
